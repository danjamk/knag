Findings

  • The conversion default is measured, not lore. RevenueCat's State of Subscription Apps 2026 (their platform's install base, tens of thousands of apps) puts median freemium download→paid at 2.1% within 35 days; hard-paywall apps convert 10.7% of a smaller funnel. The old "2–5% freemium" range is ProfitWell/OpenView-era SaaS lore. The simulator applies conversion per download — matching how the benchmark is measured — with paid renewal at 96%/month (≈61%/year, mid-range for consumer annual plans).
  • Retention, not acquisition, sets the ceiling. With a front-loaded retention curve, steady-state active users ≈ monthly downloads × 3.5 (weak) / 5 (typical) / 9 (strong). Doubling marketing effort doubles the base; moving M1 retention from 25% to 50% nearly triples it. Product quality is the highest-leverage growth input, and it is the only one that doesn't cost recurring hours.
  • Price decides viability, not infrastructure. $1.99/yr needs ~7% conversion just to cover the Cloudflare bill — more than triple the measured median. $9.99/yr breaks even at ~1.4%, below the median. Infrastructure is ~$0.10–0.15 per user per year and flat with scale; the Workers bill is never what kills this.
  • Paid acquisition can never pay back. Net lifetime revenue per download is roughly 2% × $8.49 × a few subscriber-years ≈ well under $1. Consumer CPIs run $1–5. The gap is structural, not a tuning problem — growth must be organic/guerrilla, which is operator time, not money. The effective-hourly tile is that time priced.
  • Support is the cost that can break it, and the mitigation is the product. Even 0.5% monthly contact at scale is hundreds of emails. A one-page plain-text product with AI-automated first-line support plausibly holds this near zero — but it is an assumption the model does not price.
  • The free tier must cap its own cost. The two behaviors that drive the bill — multi-device sync (the polling multiplier) and agent/MCP access — are also the most premium-feeling features. Gating them keeps a free user under half a cent a month structurally.
  • Risk profile: low stakes, honest ceiling. Downside is time (opportunity cost — the hourly tile makes it visible); upside at benchmark assumptions is beer money plus lessons and optionality, not an income. The model's job is to keep that trade explicit.

The model behind the simulator

Growth — Bass diffusion into a finite niche. New downloads each month are effort pull plus word-of-mouth pull, scaled by how much of the market remains: D(t) = (dph·hours + q·U(t−1)) × (1 − A(t−1)/M), where A is lifetime downloads and M the addressable market. The parameters are decisions, not curve-fitting knobs: M is the honest size of the niche (the input nobody wants to type), effort pull is your guerrilla hours priced in downloads, and q is the product's inherent shareability — deliberately low for a private, single-user utility.

Retention — a curve, not a rate. Downloads age through a front-loaded retention curve r(a) instead of flat churn: U(t) = Σ D(t−a)·r(a). Flat monthly churn was the previous model's biggest lie — it kept a third of a cohort alive at month 36, when real consumer utilities lose most of a cohort in the first month and then flatten:

Infrastructure. Target architecture: Cloudflare Workers + D1 (hash-sharded, 90-day revision retention — the ladder below), Workers Paid plan. Blended usage per active user: ~365 requests/day (~11K/month — adaptive polling per spec §14.4 plus saves), ~3 D1 row reads per request, ~4.5K row writes/month, ~3MB steady-state storage, plus login email.

Sensitivity. The profile above is the blended realistic case. Spec §14.4's worst case — every device, board open all day — is ~4K requests/day (~3.6×). If every user behaved that way, cost rises to ~$0.55/user-yr and $9.99 at benchmark conversion goes underwater — which is exactly why the free tier caps devices (below). The paid tier can absorb heavy use; the free tier must not be able to generate it.

The alignment is the point: the two things that cost money are the two things worth paying for. That is rare, and it means scale cannot hurt the free tier economics.

Payment economics

Apple Small Business Program: 15% commission while proceeds stay under $1M/yr — every scenario here qualifies. Standard rate is 30%. Since the US anti-steering ruling, web checkout via Stripe (2.9% + 30¢) is a legal alternative for US users. Apple Developer Program: $99/yr.

Architecture at scale — stay on Cloudflare

The current architecture is already the cheap one for this workload shape (billions of tiny requests, KB payloads, per-user data, a free static shell). Scaling is a ladder of deferred steps, each behind a tripwire, all inside the platform:

StepTripwireWhat changes
1 · Revision retention policy~1K users (append-only snapshots fill the 10GB database within a year)Prune sealed revisions past ~90 days. One store.ts change. History UX opens on “yesterday”; nothing needs forever.
2 · Hash-shard D1~10K users (~30GB steady state vs 10GB/database)4–10 databases, users hash to a shard, store.ts resolves the binding. All SQL is already behind that chokepoint — the §17 insurance paying off again.
3 · ~30–100 shards~100K users (~300GB; account holds 50K databases / 1TB)Same mechanism, more bindings. Per-user databases are the far end — chosen for tenant isolation (export, delete-by-drop), not cost.
4 · Durable Objects + WebSockets (only if the product changes)Live collaboration ambitions, or ~1M usersHibernating WebSockets replace polling with push-on-save; DO SQLite storage is $0.20/GB vs D1's $0.75. Saves only ~$500/mo at 100K — a re-platform never pays for itself on infra at these scales.

Why not AWS. The equivalent stack (Lambda + DynamoDB + Cognito + CloudFront) runs ~$1,900–2,500/mo at 100K users — roughly 2× — before ops burden. Two structural reasons: auth is a real line item on AWS (Cognito ~$500/mo at 100K MAU) and a rounding error self-hosted, and per-request gateways price this workload worst (API Gateway alone would add ~$1,100/mo at $1/M). The only futures where AWS makes sense are organizational — an acquisition, an enterprise estate — never cost or capability.
